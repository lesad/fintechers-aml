AML Triage Playbook

Banks catch money laundering with a pipeline of know-your-customer checks, screening, monitoring rules and human review. The rules are the easy part. The hard part is the flood of alerts, most of them false, that someone has to close with a written reason.

90%+ of rule-based monitoring alerts are false positives, a widely cited industry figure. Datos, as quoted by Flagright; not independently verified.
The challenge in one screen

Three red-flag rules, four things per flag

July 2026 transactions vs Q2 2026 client statistics, ~400 000 transactions, ~5 500 clients, all amounts in CZK. Volume = all of a client's transactions in both directions unless the brief says otherwise.

A

Client profile discrepancy

Daily volume > 25% of projected monthly volume, or > 3× Q2 average daily volume; or one transaction > 2× the Q2 maximum. Clients inactive in Q2: projected volumes only.

one alert per client
B

High-risk jurisdiction / PEP

Transaction > 10 000 CZK touching an EU or FATF high-risk country (payer, payee or client); or a PEP's outgoing transaction > 100 000 CZK (any direction when unclear).

one alert per transaction
C

Flow-through account

> 100 000 CZK in within 24 h, then ≥ 80% of it out within 6 h to an account that is not the payer's. Add other red flags you find.

one alert per client

Data traps

Where naive code gets the alert set wrong

Inactive in Q2

Rule A compares these clients to projected volumes only. Open: is "expected volume at contract" monthly, and is the Q2 daily average over 91 calendar days or business days? Confirm at the briefing.

Direction unclear

Rule B's PEP limit is for outgoing payments, but any direction counts when the direction is unclear.

Back to the payer

Rule C ignores money sent back to the payer's own account. Match anonymised accounts, not names.

Two windows

Rule C has a 24 h inflow window and a 6 h outflow window. Combine date and time before computing them.

No listed country in the data

The 33 countries in the data include none of the 31 on the July 2026 EU and FATF lists, so rule B's country branch fires zero times. Only the PEP branch fires (23 PEP clients). Keep the list as data and ask FINREG which list they mean.

Remittances have no payer

39 715 remittances have an empty payer account: direction unclear, so they count for the PEP branch "any transaction", and rule C can't match them to a payer.

No MCC column

The track page mentions MCC, the data has none. Profile checks use client type, contract channel, country, expected volume and the low/medium/high AML risk score.

FX units

23 currencies. Use the ČNB fixing on or before the transaction date, and divide by the quantity in the header (100 HUF, 100 JPY, 100 INR…).

From the research: market, regulators, practitioners

Ten priorities, in order

What decides the score. The build plan below turns them into steps; the Triage logic tab has the tables to encode.

  1. Make escalation your headline

    No vendor page we opened shows routing through an escalation matrix with conflict-of-interest checks and four-eyes approval; their agents stop at "escalate to a senior". The legal basis: the contact person may not conclude or settle trades or sit in audit (§ 22 of 253/2008), the decree requires separating incompatible functions (67/2018), and PEP cases need a board member's or delegate's consent (§ 9a(3)(d)). "An analyst never reviews a client they manage" is good practice (a MAS finding), not found in Czech or EU law.

  2. Never auto-close a high-risk alert

    AMLA's draft monitoring guidelines (consultation closed Sep 2026) allow automated closure only where nothing is unusual, and not for higher-risk customers. AMLR Art. 76(5) requires "meaningful human intervention". So: FP auto-close only for low-risk clients; PEP, high-risk country or high score is always REVIEW; insufficient data defaults to REVIEW, never FP.

  3. A playbook per rule, with its legal basis

    Each alert runs a fixed checklist and cites its basis: flow-through = § 6(1)(a), profile discrepancy = § 9(2)(d), high-risk country/PEP = § 9a. Vendors call this "policy-bound agents" (Oracle, Silent Eight); it's the auditable alternative to free chat.

  4. Reconstructability is the law, not a nice-to-have

    ČNB decree 67/2018 § 18: decisions, reasons, inputs and responsibilities must be reconstructable, showing who did each step, when, with what result. ČNB benchmark 2/2023 calls a closure whose reason can't be reconstructed imprudent. Hash-chained log, retention configurable (10 years in Czech law today; 5 + 5 under AMLR Art. 77).

  5. Score like the EBA says

    EBA/GL/2021/02: no single factor dominates, legally high-risk situations can't be weighted away, overrides are documented, the combination is explainable. Decree 67/2018 § 7 asks for named factors, weights and interrelations. Use a versioned scorecard, not ML (no labels).

  6. The model proposes, cites and is checked

    Preprint results (Torres et al., synthetic data) show retrieval plus a fixed output contract cuts numeric inconsistency and invented policy several-fold. Give the model an evidence bundle with IDs, demand cited IDs, reject any number not in the evidence, run a counterfactual (drop the key factor, the decision should move). ČNB's AI section: "self-explainable", not a black box.

  7. Measure two numbers

    Agreement with our own labels on a held-out set of ~25 alerts (Cohen's kappa; below 0.60 is weak), and time from transaction to decision. ČNB's benchmark starts the clock when the alert is generated and criticises backlogs of "tens to hundreds of days".

  8. Keep the country list as data

    The July 2026 lists (EU Delegated Reg. 2016/1675 as amended by 2026/46 and 2026/83, FATF June 2026) give 31 codes in reference/high-risk-countries-2026-07.json. None appears in the ČNB data, so the country branch is silent until FINREG names another list. Swapping it must be a config change, logged with version and date.

  9. Tipping-off and data handling

    Report and investigation status visible only to AML roles (§ 38; AMLR Art. 73). The detecting employee's name never enters the report payload (§ 18(3)). Mask identifiers before the model; no commercial reuse of AML data (Art. 76(4)).

  10. Pitch as an overlay

    The market sells layers on top of existing monitoring (Silent Eight, Lucinity, Verafin), not replacements. Pitch: plug into the bank's existing alerts, add triage, escalation and the reconstructable trail the ČNB already asks for.

02

Generate red flags A, B and C

B: 1–3 h · A, C: after 6 h

Goal: the exact alert set the ruleset defines, with the evidence that triggered each alert attached. Rule B first: one alert per transaction, no time windows, so the slice moves fast. A and C join once the path works.

Do

  1. A: daily volume per client per July day; compare to 25% of projected monthly and to 3× Q2 average daily; separately, each transaction to 2× Q2 maximum. Inactive in Q2: projected only. Collapse to one alert per client, keeping every triggering day as evidence.
  2. B: per transaction: amount > 10 000 CZK and payer, payee or client country on the list; or PEP outgoing > 100 000 CZK (any direction if unclear).
  3. C: per client, slide a 24 h window over incoming payments; where the sum exceeds 100 000 CZK, sum outgoing in the following 6 h to accounts that are not a payer's; flag at ≥ 80%.
  4. Write tiny hand-made fixtures per rule (5–10 rows each) with known answers, and test against them.
  5. Optional extra flags for C: amounts just under 10 000 (structuring), round sums, many small inflows from different payers, a dormant client suddenly active, a natural person with business-like volume.

Focus

This drives the 40% triage score: a wrong alert set can't be triaged correctly. Spot-check five alerts per type by hand.

Watch out

  • Rule C across midnight: calendar-day buckets miss it.
  • "Not the payer's account": compare anonymised account IDs, per inflow.
  • A client can trip A on many days: still one alert.

Done when

All fixtures pass, and alert counts per type are in the write-up.

03

Score every alert 0–100

3–6 h simple · tune after

Goal: a priority anyone can recompute by hand from a printed breakdown.

Do

  1. Use only the factors the brief names: AML risk score, PEP, country, amount or volume, conduct discrepancy, relationship length, contract channel, multiple flags.
  2. Normalise each to 0–1. Amount works best as a ratio to the client's profile, on a log scale, capped.
  3. Weighted sum to 100, plus a few hard floors (for example PEP + high-risk country ≥ 80).
  4. Store the per-factor contributions as JSON on every alert.
  5. Plot the distribution and adjust weights so the REVIEW share looks plausible per type.

Focus

Correctness is judged on 30–45 sampled alerts. Read the top and bottom 10 per type and ask "would an AML officer agree?"

Watch out

  • Weights that no one can explain will cost justification points.
  • Don't double-count: B already means a high-risk country.

Done when

Every alert has a score and a breakdown, and the weights table is in the write-up.

04

Triage: decide in code, explain with AI

3–6 h · slice

Goal: a decision made by code and a specific, cited, number-backed reason for every alert. The model explains; it never decides a mandatory rule.

Do

  1. Ask typed questions per alert: is the activity consistent with the client's profile (client type, channel, country, expected volume, AML risk score), which typology fits rule C (payroll pass-through, own-account sweep, trade settlement, mule pattern, other), severity 1 to 5. Each answer comes with a probability.
  2. Code decides FP or REVIEW from the score band plus those rules. Weak evidence or high risk always goes to a human.
  3. Get typed answers from a model with structured output. Ideas in the team notes: Jev (TypeSafe SDK) for the typed decisions, openai.gpt-oss-120b-1:0 on Bedrock for the justification, Claude Haiku 4.5 through the eu. profile as fallback. Whatever the choice, Bedrock schemas can't enforce numeric ranges, so code range-checks.
  4. The justification cites transaction ids; code checks that every cited id exists and belongs to the alert. If Jev and gpt-oss disagree on triage, or confidence is low, the alert goes to review.
  5. Guardrails in and out: mask account IDs and names before the model; block injected instructions; reject a reason that cites a number not in the evidence (grounding check).
  6. Log the model, prompt hash, retrieved passages and raw output for every AI-touched alert.

Focus

Justification is 25% at FINREG and explainability sits in the main jury's responsible-AI 15%. A good reason names the rule, the number, the comparison, the context and the source.

Watch out

  • If the model disagrees with code, it can only flag it for a human, never flip it.
  • Temperature 0 and a fixed prompt, or reruns won't match.

Done when

No alert lacks a cited reason, one guardrail can be triggered on demand, and a teammate has read 15 reasons cold.

05

Escalate, open a case, refuse the unsafe one

3–6 h · slice

Goal: every alert has an executive and an approver allowed by the matrix, a case is really opened, and a conflicted assignment is visibly refused.

Do

  1. Type the escalation matrix into a data file. Until the briefing gives the official one, a draft from the team notes: A → L1 then L2 analyst; B → L2 then AML compliance officer, PEP cases also the board member responsible for AML; C → L2 then contact person, with an OPO to FAÚ and a § 20 hold of 24 h by default.
  2. Write conflict rules as functions that return true or false: approver ≠ preparer, no relationship-manager link to the client, not in front office or internal audit. Model the roles as configurable: under AMLR the board member becomes the compliance manager and the reporter the compliance officer.
  3. Pick the first eligible person per role, then the approver from the next level up.
  4. Real action: for each REVIEW alert, write a case file to S3 (evidence, score, reason, assignees) and notify the executive. This is the "case opened" the kickoff asked for.
  5. Guardrail: when the first candidate is conflicted, refuse, log the reason and pick the next one. This is your demo's refusal moment.
  6. Write one test per matrix row and per conflict rule, plus a check across all alerts that executive ≠ approver.

Focus

The cheapest 20% at FINREG, and it gives the main jury the "real action" and "guardrail shown" proofs for free.

Watch out

  • PEP cases often need senior management by law; check the matrix.
  • If nobody is eligible, escalate one level up and log why, rather than breaking a rule.

Done when

All tests pass and the cross-alert check finds zero violations.

Extra red flags worth adding

  • Structuring: ≥ 3 transactions in 7 days at 90–100% of a rule threshold.
  • Fan-in / fan-out: unique payers or payees in 24 h or 7 days vs the Q2 count.
  • Round amounts: share divisible by 1 000 / 10 000 above the population's 95th percentile.
  • Dormant or new, then active: contract < 90 days or Q2 count ≈ 0, July above projection.
  • Test payment: small payment to a new payee, then ≥ 10× to the same payee within 48 h.

Pitch it on the 2027 deadline

Pitch lines

  • Why now: "From July 2027 every Czech bank, broker and payment institution re-checks its high-risk clients within a year, under a wider PEP definition, with a named manager accountable. Analysts don't scale; triage does."
  • Who pays: obliged entities' compliance budgets: banks, payment and e-money institutions, investment firms, crypto providers. Smaller ones first; they can't hire their way through the wave.
  • Value metric: analyst hours saved per 1 000 alerts, and time to produce an audit-ready rationale.
  • Moat: regulation encoded as data (thresholds, lists, matrix), so a rule change is a config change with a log entry, not a project.

What to show to make it stick

  • A "regulation switch" in the demo: flip the PEP definition or a threshold from today's value to the AMLR value and show the alert count and queue change, with the change itself logged.
  • On the one-pager: "AMLR-ready: 1-year/5-year review cycles, extended PEP, compliance manager trail."
  • Mention it in one sentence in the 4-minute demo; FINREG is a regulatory law firm and ČNB is on the jury, so they will notice.

Responsible-AI one-pager

  • AI Act tier (the kickoff asked for it): not listed as high-risk in Annex III (AML is not named; 5(b) excludes fraud detection from credit scoring); not prohibited profiling under Art. 5(1)(d), because it supports a human's assessment on objective facts; high-risk-grade practices applied voluntarily; reassess before Annex III obligations apply on 2 Dec 2027 (Reg. 2026/1744). Have a lawyer check the wording.
  • Human intervention: closing high-risk alerts, filing a report or changing due-diligence measures is done by named people with four eyes, matching AMLR Art. 76(5) and GDPR Art. 22.

What a regulator or lawyer judge will check

  1. Each flag cites its legal or typology basis.
  2. Reconstructable log: inputs, rule version, score, who, when, outcome.
  3. Every closure says why suspicion was excluded, and any follow-up.
  4. No automated closure for PEP, high-risk country or high score; sampled QA of auto-closed ones.
  5. Risk-based priority; SLA clock from alert generation; backlog visible.
  6. Named factors, weights, interactions; versioned; no black box.
  7. AI drafts and navigates rules; deterministic rules and a human decide.
  8. Thresholds justified per segment; whitelists with reason and review date.
  9. Preparer ≠ approver; contact person outside front office and audit (§ 22). "RM never decides on own client" is practice, not Czech or EU law.
  10. PEP and high-risk-country cases go to the statutory-body delegate.
  11. Conflict checks and overrides are logged.
  12. Tipping-off protection; detecting employee kept out of the report.
  13. A 24 h postponement path for urgent cases (§ 20).
  14. Country lists versioned with source and date, EU and FATF kept apart.
  15. Data minimisation, masking before the model, every LLM call logged.
Regulation as the pitch

AMLR 2027 makes triage capacity the bottleneck

From 10 July 2027 the EU AML Regulation (2024/1624) replaces most of the Czech AML act (253/2008 Sb.) and applies directly. It lowers thresholds, widens the PEP definition, fixes review cycles and binds the ČNB and FAÚ to EU-wide standards. Every change means more checks, more alerts and more decisions that must be explained. That is the "why now" for a triage tool.
